# Computational Methods

## System Setup and Box Construction

### Construction of the probe-solvent cells

Each model system consisted of a single target molecule (hereafter the probe, OPA) dissolved in either a pure solvent or a binary solvent mixture. The probe was taken from *structures/opa.vasp* and treated as configurationally rigid. In every system it was placed at the centre of a periodic cubic cell and kept fixed for the whole simulation, so the dynamics that were probed reflected only the behaviour of the surrounding liquid, with no contribution from translation or rotation of the solute itself.

### Packing of the solvent around the probe

The solvent was introduced around the fixed probe with Packmol. Molecule counts for each component were derived from the experimental bulk density of the solvent and its molar mass: the requested volume fraction for a given component was converted into the corresponding number of molecules in the cell, so the box reproduced the nominal composition on a volume basis. For the binary mixtures examined here (acetone/n-heptane and related compositions), the two components were packed to the stated volume fractions, and the cell edge was set in the range 45-50 &Aring; so that each system contained on the order of a few thousand atoms. A packing tolerance of 2.2&Aring; and a margin of 2.0&Aring; were used to prevent unrealistically short contacts, both between molecules and between the solvent and the cell walls. The conventional rigid-box Packmol procedure was used, with periodic boundary conditions applied in all three dimensions.

## Interatomic Potential: a Refined MACE Model

### Choice of a machine-learned potential

All forces and potential energies were obtained from a machine-learned interatomic potential based on the MACE architecture. Rather than using the pretrained model as released, it was fine-tuned on first-principles reference data to improve its accuracy for the interatomic interactions that dominate the response of the probe.

### Fine-tuning on first-principles data

The reference set was generated by density functional theory calculations, and the refinement targeted the accurate reproduction of atomic forces. This choice is deliberate: it is the force field that controls the short-time dynamics of the solvent and, in turn, the mechanical load experienced by the probe. The fine-tuning step gives the description of interactions a higher fidelity than the original, generic training of the model, bringing the potential surface closer to the underlying electronic-structure surface for the relevant chemistry and energy range.

## Molecular Dynamics Protocol

### Thermostatting and initial conditions

Classical molecular dynamics simulations were carried out with the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) using a Langevin thermostat. The probe was held fixed, and the solvent was allowed to evolve under the forces furnished by the refined MACE model. Initial atomic velocities were assigned from a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution at 300 K, and the overall linear and angular momentum of the system were removed before production started.

### Integration settings

The equations of motion were integrated with a time step of 1.0 fs. The Langevin thermostat kept the temperature at 300 K, with a friction coefficient of 0.01 fs<sup>-1</sup>. This gives a reasonably soft coupling, so the dynamics retain a physical character while still converging to the target temperature on a short time scale. Trajectories were propagated for a total of 10 ps, with frames written every 0.1 ps for later analysis. Throughout the run the cell vectors were held fixed and periodic boundary conditions applied.

## Analysis of Probe Displacement and Force

### Observables extracted from the trajectory

The central observables of the study were the net force exerted on the fixed probe by the surrounding solvent and the displacement of its centre of mass relative to its initial position.

At each saved frame the total net force acting on the probe was computed as the vector sum of the per-atom forces on all atoms of the probe, and its magnitude |**F**| was recorded. Because the probe is held fixed, this net force is the instantaneous mechanical load that the solvent applies to the molecule, and its magnitude gives a direct measure of how strongly the liquid perturbs the probe. In the SAM-growth context, a large sustained force is undesirable: it would tend to disturb an ordered, densely packed self-assembled monolayer once the molecules are attached.

The displacement of the probe centre of mass, **d**(t) = **R**<sub>COM</sub>(t) - **R**<sub>COM</sub>(0), together with its norm |**d**|, was evaluated from the same trajectory. For a fixed probe this quantity is dominated by the thermal and packing fluctuations of the surrounding solvent sampled over the short trajectories collected here. It is therefore interpreted as an indicator of the mobility and configurational sampling that a given solvent offers to a solute, bearing in mind the inherent statistical character of the displacement in finite, short runs.

## Systems Studied

### Overview of the solvent environments

Three classes of systems were examined, corresponding to different solvent environments:

1. **Pure solvents**, in which the probe was surrounded by a single component: acetone, acetonitrile, ethanol, methanol, n-heptane, propanol (prol), THF, and toluene.
2. **Acetone/n-heptane mixtures**, spanning acetone volume fractions from 1 % to 40 % (with 50 % prepared as an additional point in the workflow), which allowed the solvent composition to be varied while n-heptane remained the dominant, least-perturbing component.
3. **Representative binary mixtures at a fixed 5:95 composition** (acetone/n-heptane, THF/n-heptane, THF/toluene, and toluene/n-heptane), used to compare the effect of the minor component on the probe response.

### Criterion for favourable solvent conditions

For every system the probe force and centre-of-mass displacement were extracted along the trajectory and, where appropriate, the average force was compared across compositions. This was done to identify the solvent conditions that minimise the perturbation of the probe - a criterion relevant to the formation of well-ordered self-assembled monolayers.
